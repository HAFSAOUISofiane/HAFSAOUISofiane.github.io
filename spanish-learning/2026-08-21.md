# Día 22 (2026-08-21): El puente y la lluvia

## Texto

Este fin de semana hay puente, y Marta y yo hemos decidido hacer una excursión a la sierra, aunque el pronóstico no es muy bueno. «Aunque diga que va a llover, yo me animo a ir», comenta Marta. «Prefiero mojarme un poco a quedarme en casa». Yo no estoy tan seguro, pero al final decido acompañarla: hay que aprovechar el puente, porque llevamos meses sin salir del pueblo.

Salimos temprano y, durante la primera hora, hace un sol estupendo. Pero, como habían anunciado, empieza un chubasco fuerte a media mañana. Como no llevamos paraguas, nos refugiamos bajo un árbol grande. «Menos mal que traje el impermeable», dice Marta, mientras yo me quedo completamente empapado.

Cuando para de llover, seguimos el paseo y comemos algo en un chiringuito de montaña. Al final, aunque volvemos a casa un poco mojados, los dos estamos de acuerdo: ha merecido la pena. Aunque llueva la próxima vez, pienso animarme otra vez a salir.

## Vocabulario nuevo

1. **aunque** (conj) — although / even if — *Aunque el pronóstico no es muy bueno...* — No direct French cognate; French uses "bien que" (+ subjonctif) or "même si" (+ indicatif) depending on the same real/hypothetical distinction you'll see below.
2. **el pronóstico** (n) — forecast — *El pronóstico no es muy bueno.* — True cognate of French "le pronostic" — an easy one.
3. **animarse (a)** (v, reflexive) — to work up the motivation, to decide to (do something) — *Yo me animo a ir.* — Partial false friend vs. French "s'animer" (to come to life, to get lively/busy) — related root, but the Spanish sense of "talking yourself into it" isn't there in French.
4. **mojarse** (v, reflexive) — to get wet — *Prefiero mojarme un poco.* — No French cognate (French: "se mouiller", different Latin root); don't confuse with "mojar" (to dip/wet something).
5. **aprovechar** (v) — to make the most of / take advantage of — *Hay que aprovechar el puente.* — Visual false friend vs. French "approcher" (to approach) — looks similar, unrelated meaning; French for "to take advantage of" is "profiter de".
6. **el chubasco** (n) — (sudden) rain shower — *Empieza un chubasco fuerte.* — No French cognate (French: "l'averse").
7. **el paraguas** (n) — umbrella — *No llevamos paraguas.* — No French cognate word-for-word, but a fun structural parallel: "para-aguas" (for water) mirrors French "para-pluie" (for rain) — same compound logic, different noun.
8. **refugiarse** (v, reflexive) — to take shelter — *Nos refugiamos bajo un árbol.* — True cognate of French "se réfugier".
9. **el impermeable** (n) — raincoat — *Traje el impermeable.* — True cognate of French "l'imperméable".
10. **empapado / empapada** (adj) — soaked, drenched — *Me quedo completamente empapado.* — No French cognate (French: "trempé").
11. **la excursión** (n) — outing, day trip — *Hacer una excursión a la sierra.* — True cognate of French "l'excursion".
12. **merecer la pena** (expr) — to be worth it — *Ha merecido la pena.* — No French cognate (French: "ça valait le coup" / "ça valait la peine" — the second is actually a near-match, just check the different verb: "valoir" vs. "merecer").

## Grammar focus

**1. Aunque + indicativo vs. subjuntivo — concession (known fact vs. hypothetical)**

You've already learned that the subjunctive shows up after wishes (Day 19), impersonal opinions (Day 20), and doubt (Day 21). Today adds a fourth, very common trigger: **aunque** ("although" / "even if"), where the mood you choose actually changes the meaning:

- **Aunque + indicativo** = a known, real fact. *Aunque llueve, salimos.* (It IS raining — that's a fact — and we're going out anyway.)
- **Aunque + subjuntivo** = a hypothetical, not-yet-confirmed, or simply unimportant condition. *Aunque llueva, salimos.* (Whether or not it rains — I don't know / it doesn't matter — we're going out.)

In the text: *"Aunque diga que va a llover, yo me animo a ir"* uses the subjunctive (*diga*) because the forecast is uncertain — it's a prediction, not a confirmed fact. Compare that to the very last line, *"Aunque volvemos a casa un poco mojados..."*, which uses the indicative (*volvemos*) because by that point in the story, getting wet is simply a fact that already happened. This real-vs-hypothetical logic is the same one you already use for *cuando* (Day 20) — French, by contrast, mostly reaches for different words ("bien que" vs. "même si") rather than switching mood on the same word, so lean on the logic, not on a French reflex.

**2. Imperativo negativo (repaso de los subjuntivos)**

Quick, useful side note: the negative command form ("don't do...") for **usted** — and for all persons, in fact — simply reuses the present subjunctive forms you already know, with **no** in front. You've met *tenga, haga, ponga, sea, esté*, etc. (Day 21) as subjunctive forms after "que"; the exact same forms become negative commands:

- *No se preocupe.* (Don't worry. — from *preocuparse*, subjunctive *se preocupe*)
- *No lo haga así.* (Don't do it like that.)
- *No sea tan pesimista.* (Don't be so pessimistic.)

So there's no new conjugation to memorize here — just a new job for a form you already have.

## Quick review (spaced repetition)

- **Dudar / no creer que + subjuntivo** — doubt or denied certainty triggers the subjunctive, e.g. *dudo que podamos terminarlo* (Day 21).
- **cuando + subjuntivo** — for a future, not-yet-real event, e.g. *cuando empiece el curso...* (Day 20).
- **el puente** — long weekend (lit. "bridge"); near-perfect calque of French "faire le pont" (earlier lesson).
- **la previsión del tiempo** — weather forecast — today's "el pronóstico" is a common synonym you'll hear just as often.
- **el chiringuito** — casual beach/seaside food stall; today reused loosely for a "chiringuito de montaña" (mountain snack bar) (earlier lesson).
